# Group orders: a teardown of what managers built

*"Make" evidence for area 11: the tools and rules K-pop group order managers created to run their orders. Last updated November 9, 2026.*

## What was studied

The public pages of two group order managers (GOMs). Both are anonymized here, following the consent rules in the research kit, even though their pages are public. Identifying details are generalized and quotes paraphrased:

- **Manager A:** a small team in North America running group orders, buying and selling, using a Korean address shared by a fellow manager. Terms several years old.
- **Manager B:** a manager in North America who ran dozens of formal group orders over several years, then stopped. Records stay public.

*Both pages are a few years old, and practices may have changed. Two pages are a small sample. Treat this as a first map to test in interviews, not a finding.*

## How an order runs

Both managers use the same three-payment structure:

1. **First payment: the item.** Paid *before* filling in the order form. Manager A require the PayPal transaction ID in the form, or the form is deleted.
2. **Second payment: international shipping,** split among joiners once the order closes and the real cost is known. Manager B splits by each joiner's share of the item value; Manager A also bill separate "fee payments" for Korean domestic shipping, bank transfer, PayPal and proxy fees.
3. **Third payment: domestic shipping** from the manager to the joiner. Manager A give 30 days, or up to two months for combined shipping; local pick-ups skip this payment.

**Deadlines are short and enforced.** Manager B gives one week per payment. Manager A give 72 hours for the second payment, then charge $1 a day for up to a week, after which the joiner is dropped with no refund and their items are offered to others.

## The tools they built

| Tool | What it does | Who uses it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Carrd page | Rules, FAQ, shipping policy, disclaimers | Both |
| Google Forms | Order intake; the automatic submission email *is* the order confirmation | Both |
| Public spreadsheet per order | Joiners' initials, items, costs and payment status, color-coded (red for missing, orange for incorrect) | Manager B |
| Masterlist | Status of every order ("at Korean address," "begun domestic delivery," "completed") | Both |
| X hashtags | Separate tags for orders, updates, feedback, buying, selling, trading, interest checks, forwards and domestic shipping | Manager A |
| Email | Payment requests for the second and third payments, and tracking numbers | Both |
| Anonymous feedback form with public responses | Reputation | Manager B |
| Strikes and blacklists | Enforcement against late or wrong payments | Both |

## How money moves, and where it hurts

**Payment rails are dictated, not chosen.** Both insist on PayPal Friends & Family, in US dollars. Manager A also accept a local bank transfer and, for local joiners only, cash. If a joiner pays with Goods & Services (with buyer protection), Manager A refund it and keep $0.30. Manager B allows it if the joiner pays the extra fee, noting that PayPal's protection only lasts 180 days anyway, while orders can take longer. Joiners in countries where only Goods & Services works (Mexico is the example) have to ask for a different price.

**Matching payments to people is manual.** Joiners must add notes to every payment. A missing note earns a strike, and the money is held until the joiner explains. Proof of payment means copying a PayPal transaction ID into a form.

**Managers carry the cash-flow risk.** Manager A say that covering fees in advance became too expensive, so they now bill them right after paying for orders. Late fees exist because the manager is covering the joiner's share in the meantime. Joiners can't pay everything up front either, because the costs aren't known yet.

**Currency conversion is a hidden cost.** Manager A add conversion and bank transfer fees on top of the converted price and warn that prices will look higher than what Google shows.

**Joiners have little protection.** No refunds for dropping out. If someone takes over a spot found by the manager, the original joiner gets back only 25%. If the seller turns out to be a scammer, the manager disclaims any further responsibility.

**Speed of payment is a reward.** When photocards and other extras are sorted among joiners, Manager A give first pick to whoever paid the international shipping fastest.

**It wears managers out.** Manager A mention an earlier account they stopped for lack of time. Manager B stopped after several years. Both pages spend much of their text deflecting questions ("check the spreadsheet first") and disclaiming responsibility.

## Patterns worth testing

1. **The core job is bookkeeping, not payment.** Managers spend their effort matching payments to people, tracking status and chasing deadlines. The payment itself is the easy part.
2. **Fees are avoided at the cost of trust.** Friends & Family is cheap and fast, so both sides give up protection. Something that is cheap *and* gives joiners proof and some recourse would change the trade.
3. **Staged payments with unknown amounts are built in.** Any tool has to handle "pay now, more later, amount to be announced."
4. **Reputation is public and built by hand:** feedback hashtags, public feedback forms, strikes and blacklists.
5. **Cross-border joiners pay more:** conversion fees, Goods & Services surcharges, sometimes exclusion.
6. **Managers burn out.** Reducing their admin load may matter more than anything offered to joiners.

## What this suggests could serve them

*Neutral; product-specific ideas belong in strategy documents.*

- **Payment requests that carry the order reference,** so payments match themselves to joiners and stages.
- **A shared status view** that updates as payments arrive, replacing the color-coded spreadsheet.
- **Staged requests** for amounts that are only known later.
- **Receipts and proof** that joiners keep, as an alternative to buyer protection.
- **Portable reputation** for managers: completed orders and feedback attached to their name.
- **Settlement in the manager's currency,** without hidden conversion markups.
